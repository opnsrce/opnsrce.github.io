title: Cooking Pasta Like Risotto

Recently, I gave Ethan Chlebowski's "risotto" [technique][01] for cooking pasta
a try. Basically, instead of cooking the pasta in quarts of boiling liquid, you
cook the pasta in a wide, shallow vessel, adding just enough liquid to cover the
pasta.

When I first saw the video I was skeptical: Ethan cooks a fairly small amount of
pasta in a massive vessel (a wok). I had doubts that you could use this
technique to cook a standard pound of dried pasta in a large skillet.

I was able to make it work, with a few tweaks.

In a large, high-walled skillet, set the heat to medium. Heat 1-2 tablespoons
of vegetable oil in the pan. When the oil is hot, add your pasta. This ensures
the pasta gets evenly coated with the oil and prevents it from burning.

Once your pasta has achieved a nice toasted color, turn the heat to high and add
your cooking liquid. In my tests, it took about 1.5 quarts of liquid to cook 1
pound of pasta. The amount of liquid should *barely* cover the pasta in the pan.
Once the liquid comes up to a boil, continuously stir the pasta in the pan,
pushing down any pieces that try to float above the liquid.

After about 5-8 minutes, most of the liquid should be absorbed by the pasta
and the pasta itself should be al dente or near it. If the liquid is low and the
pasta still isn't done, you can add more to the pan roughly a third of a cup at
a time.

The key is to stir the pasta aggressively, making sure it absorbs as much liquid
as possible from the pan. You'll know it's done when the pasta is al dente and
the left over liquid in the pan has turned into a thick sauce thanks to the
starches in the pasta.

Take the pasta off the heat. Now's your chance to add whatever you want to
flavor the pasta.

I recommend a shitload of cheese.

I stirred in a cup of finely grated parmesano regiano cheese directly into the
pasta. The remaining liquid and residual heat turned the cheese into a glossy
sauce that beautifully coated the pasta. The fact that the pasta was toasted at
the start gave it a nice depth of flavor, and building the sauce directly in the
pan means the cheese emulsion is much more stable and won't split when the pasta
is reheated.

This is definitely how I'll be making pasta going forward.
